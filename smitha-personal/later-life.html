Five questions worth asking.

Tap a question for a little context. These are educational prompts, not personalized recommendations.

What kind of help would I want if daily tasks became difficult?

Think through staying at home, transportation, meal preparation, mobility and help with daily activities. Your preferences matter, even if no care is needed today.

Who could make decisions and how would they know my preferences?

Choose people you trust and discuss your preferences before an emergency. A California attorney can explain advance health-care directives and financial powers of attorney and how to name backups.

Which services are medical versus ongoing personal care?

Medical treatment and ongoing help with bathing, dressing or daily activities are not the same service. Medicare generally does not pay for most custodial long-term care; verify the type of support and funding available.

What are the likely financial and family trade-offs of different settings?

Compare home modifications, family time, paid home help and residential settings. Think about who will coordinate care as well as direct costs and the effect on family responsibilities.

Does my estate plan explain the right permissions and responsibilities?

Ask whether your existing directives and authorizations clearly identify who can act, when, and for which decisions. Also review where the documents and contact information can be found.

Keep in mind

Care costs, availability, and eligibility vary. Review official Medicare information and talk with qualified health, legal and financial professionals.

Don't leave out the essentials.

As life changes, revisit who can act for you and how your wishes will be carried out.
