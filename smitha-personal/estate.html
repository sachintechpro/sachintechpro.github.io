Make your wishes easier to follow

An estate plan is as much about who can help you while you are alive as what happens to your belongings later. Make the important decisions visible before anyone needs to guess.

Four things to understand

Start with clarity.

An estate plan is not automatically complete because a document was signed. Ownership, beneficiaries, funding, and accessibility matter.

01

Decisions while you are here

Think about who could handle money matters or communicate health-care wishes if you could not.

02

People who matter

Consider caregivers for minor children, successor decision-makers, and beneficiaries.

03

Property and accounts

Make an organized list of major assets, ownership arrangements, and where documents are kept.

04

Review after life changes

Marriage, a child, home purchase, or loss in the family may justify reviewing your documents.

Bring these to a conversation

Five questions worth asking.

Tap a question for a little context. These are educational prompts, not personalized recommendations.

Who could make health and financial decisions if I became unable to?

Financial powers of attorney and advance health-care directives serve different purposes. Think about a trusted person for each role, a backup, and how they would access the instructions when needed.

Do my will, beneficiary designations, property titles, and intentions work together?

A will, account beneficiary form, and the legal title to property can direct different assets in different ways. Ask a California estate attorney to identify inconsistencies before assuming one document controls everything.

Would a living trust help, and which assets would need to be transferred into it?

A living trust may be useful in some situations, but it is not mandatory. If an attorney recommends one, ask how appropriate assets are legally transferred into it and how completion will be checked.

Who would support minor children or other dependents?

For minor children, discuss proposed guardians with a California estate attorney and the people you hope will serve. Consider both day-to-day caregiving and how money for a child would be managed.

What should I ask an estate attorney to prepare or review?

Bring a broad list of assets, existing documents, family relationships, beneficiaries, and your chosen decision-makers. Ask what documents are needed, who prepares them, how assets are coordinated, and when reviews should occur.
