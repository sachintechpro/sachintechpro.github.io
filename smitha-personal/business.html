Protect what you are building

A small business is personal: your time, customers, workers, and family income may all depend on it. Understand what would interrupt operations and what a practical backup plan might look like.

Five questions worth asking.

Tap a question for a little context. These are educational prompts, not personalized recommendations.

What would happen if I could not run the business for several weeks?

Identify the decisions, customer promises, payments and approvals that currently depend on you. Create a short emergency sequence for who will step in and how operations can continue.

Which customers, employees and partners rely on us most?

Map critical customers, employees, suppliers and shared systems. Prioritize the relationships that would suffer most quickly if your availability or revenue changed.

What resources or protections already exist for a disruption?

Review cash reserves, written operating procedures, backup access, insurance provisions and existing business agreements. Confirm what these arrangements actually cover before assuming protection.

Who would have authority to keep the business operating?

Separate operational backup from legal authority: a colleague may know the work but lack permission to sign or access accounts. Discuss emergency authorization and succession documents with qualified professionals.

How should ownership, beneficiaries and estate planning align?

Business ownership may be affected by partnership agreements, beneficiary arrangements and estate documents. Ask your attorney and accountant how changes in ownership, taxes, succession and family obligations fit together.

Keep in mind

This is educational and not business insurance, legal, tax, or accounting advice. Eligibility and contract terms require qualified review.
